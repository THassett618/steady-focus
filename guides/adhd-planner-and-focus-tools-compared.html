ADHD planners and focus tools compared: which type fits how you get stuck

Updated 2026-10-16. Disclosure: we sell one of the items below (the ADHD Focus Bundle, plus two related kits). We have not tested the apps and other products named here. What we say about them comes from the public pages and posts linked in each section, and we flag where we are guessing.

If you have ever bought an ADHD planner and dropped it within a week, the question is rarely "which brand". It is "which kind of tool fits the way I get stuck". This guide compares the main kinds, says when each is the better pick, and is honest about where our own printables are not.

How we compared them

Four questions, kept simple:

  • Upkeep: how much work does it take to keep it current?
  • Where you get stuck: planning, starting, or tracking time?
  • Reminders: does it nudge you, or only sit there?
  • Cost: what we could confirm. We list a price only where a source we read gave one.

1. Large paper planners

Best for: people who enjoy planning and will fill it in daily.

A big planner gives you room to write everything in one place. The cost is upkeep. One blogger who tried several described a $35 "ADHD Planner" as "pages and pages of work ahead" and a six-month daily planner at $18.99 as having an overwhelming setup, in their review. That is one person's experience, not a verdict on every planner. Another writer at Neurodivergent Notes makes the broader point that a planner that is a lot of work to keep up is easy to abandon.

Choose it if you already know you stick with detailed systems.

2. Task and time apps

Best for: people who want reminders, a visual timeline, or help breaking tasks into steps.

Apps such as Tiimo aim at exactly the things paper cannot do. One App Store reviewer wrote that they "very much appreciate the breakdown tasks function and the initial suggestions for setting up a daily schedule". Another reviewer wanted more insistent reminders, so what suits you will depend on how you respond to notifications. We have not used these apps ourselves.

Choose it if you want alarms and are comfortable living in your phone.

3. Sticky notes and phone lists

Best for: today, for free.

There is nothing to buy and nothing to learn. The risk, as Neurodivergent Notes describes, is that information ends up in many places and gets updated "sporadically".

Choose it if you want to start now and can live with the scatter.

Best for: people who drop big planners and want paper.

Which one to pick, by where you get stuck

  • "I can't keep up a planner." Try the small-pages approach, or just sticky notes for a week to see what you actually use.
  • "I know what to do but can't start." Write the first physical move and try a five-minute start. You can do that on paper or in an app.
  • "I lose track of time." Pad your estimates and set a stop alarm. One clinic's write-up suggests adding 50% to every estimate (Talkiatry). A phone timer is free for that.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best ADHD planner?

There is no single best one. The best tool is the one you will still be using in a month, which usually means the one with the least upkeep for your situation.

Are paper or digital tools better for ADHD?

Neither is better for everyone. Paper has no notifications but also no reminders. Apps can remind you but live on a device full of distractions. Many people use both.

Do I need to buy anything?

No. Sticky notes and a phone timer cover a lot. Paid tools save you setup time, and that is all they do.

Are your printables medical advice?

No. They are organising tools and not a replacement for support from a clinician.
